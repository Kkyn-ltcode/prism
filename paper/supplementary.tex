\section{Experimental Setup, Training Protocol, and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:sm_hyperparameters}
\label{sm:setup}

\input{supplementary_hyperparameters.tex}

\paragraph{Training Protocol and Optimization Dynamics.}
All baseline and proposed architectures share a standardized training protocol to ensure rigorous iso-capacity evaluation (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters}). Optimization is conducted using Adam ($\beta_1 = 0.9, \beta_2 = 0.999, \epsilon = 10^{-8}$) with an initial learning rate of $1 \times 10^{-3}$, cosine annealing decay down to $\eta_{\mathrm{min}} = 10^{-5}$ across training epochs, $L_2$ weight decay of $10^{-5}$, and global gradient clipping at a maximum $L_2$ norm of $5.0$.

For standard benchmark evaluations ($N = 1{,}000$), models are trained for $15$ epochs with mini-batch size $16$; for full-scale QM9 experiments ($N = 110{,}000$), models are trained for $20$ epochs with mini-batch size $64$. Training uses a fixed epoch budget without early stopping; final evaluation uses the model checkpoint that achieved the lowest Mean Squared Error (MSE) on the validation partition ($250$ molecules for standard benchmarks, $10{,}000$ molecules for full QM9). All regression targets $y$ are standardized per target using training set statistics ($y_{\mathrm{norm}} = (y - \mu)/\sigma$) and inverse-transformed for test evaluation, reporting Mean Absolute Error (MAE) in physical units (eV for electronic energies, Debye for dipole moments, and Bohr$^3$ for polarizability).

\paragraph{Geometric Graph Construction and Radial Encodings.}
Molecular graphs $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$ are constructed by placing edges between atom pairs with Euclidean distance $r_{ij} \le 5.0$\,\AA, supplemented with a $k$-nearest neighbor fallback ($k = 8$) to guarantee connectivity for isolated peripheral atoms. Interatomic distances are encoded using $K = 8$ Gaussian Radial Basis Functions (RBF):
\begin{equation}
    \phi_k(d_{ij}) = \exp\left(-\gamma_k (d_{ij} - \mu_k)^2\right), \quad k \in \{1, \dots, K\}
\end{equation}
where $d_{ij} = \|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|^2$ is the squared Euclidean distance, with centers $\mu_k$ evenly spaced across $[0, 10]$\,\AA$^2$. Because $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$\,\AA, the RBF centers intentionally concentrate radial resolution within the primary covalent and hydrogen bonding shells ($\le 3.16$\,\AA), where orbital overlaps are most sensitive, while the broader $5.0$\,\AA\ graph radius cutoff preserves the long-range topology of the molecular scaffold.

\paragraph{Computational Hardware and Profiling Environments.}
Standard model training and benchmark sweeps were executed on NVIDIA A100 (40GB) GPUs. To isolate algorithmic complexity from hardware acceleration and eliminate GPU kernel dispatch variance during wall-time scaling profiling (SM~F), Q-PRISM PennyLane statevector simulations and PRISM classical forward/backward passes were profiled on an isolated single CPU core (AMD EPYC 7742 @ 2.25\,GHz).

\paragraph{Code and Data Availability.}
All source code for Q-PRISM, PRISM, and the complete experimental evaluation pipeline (training loops, analytical noise modeling, statistical significance testing, and visualization scripts) will be made publicly available in an open-source repository upon publication. The QM9 benchmark dataset~\cite{ramakrishnan2014quantum} is publicly accessible via PyTorch Geometric. The MD17 conformational dataset~\cite{chmielaMachineLearningAccurate2016} and 5-particle Coulomb N-body trajectory simulations are generated using established protocols documented in the literature.

\section{Statistical Methodology, Power Analysis, and Multiplicity Control}
\label{sm:fdr}
\label{sm:seed_protocol}
\label{sec:sm_fdr_table}

\paragraph{Random Seed Protocol and Variance Decomposition.}
All experimental random seeds ($42$--$46$ for 5-seed benchmarks; $42$--$61$ for 20-seed ablations) independently govern both the initialization of trainable network weights and the pseudo-random partition of dataset molecules into training, validation, and test splits. Consequently, the reported standard deviations capture composite empirical variance across optimization landscapes and molecular data sampling. The stability of effect sizes across 20 independent splits (Cohen's $d > 0.85$ on HOMO) establishes that performance differences reflect intrinsic architectural properties rather than sample partitioning artifacts.

\paragraph{Statistical Power and Non-Parametric Testing Framework.}
In the 5-seed benchmarks, non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests serve as indicators of directional sign consistency across splits (where unanimous agreement yields the minimum achievable two-sided value $p = 0.0625$ at $n=5$). Formal non-parametric hypothesis testing is powered by the 20-seed ablation (main text Section~\ref{main:sec:mechanism}), which provides sufficient statistical power to resolve rank-based significance at $\alpha = 0.05$. Post-hoc power analyses confirm that the 5-seed protocol ($n = 5$, paired) achieves $80\%$ statistical power ($1-\beta = 0.80$) for large effect sizes of Cohen's $d \ge 1.53$ at $\alpha = 0.05$, whereas the 20-seed protocol achieves $80\%$ power for medium-large effects ($d \ge 0.66$), matching the observed effect size ($d = 0.85$) for the two-qubit entanglement ablation on HOMO.

\paragraph{Multiplicity Control Framework.}
A critical methodological requirement in multi-target molecular benchmarking is controlling for false discoveries arising from multiple hypothesis testing. Evaluating 8 model architectures across 6 physical targets yields $m = 8 \times 6 = 48$ pairwise statistical comparisons, wherein each architecture is compared against its designated classical baseline (Classical Twin $\tanh$-MLP) via paired two-tailed $t$-tests across 5 random seeds. The 48 hypotheses encompass all pairwise model-vs-baseline comparisons across the full QM9 target spectrum (HOMO, LUMO, Gap, Dipole Moment, Polarizability, and Internal Energy $U_0$).

To prevent false discovery inflation while maintaining statistical power, we report two multiplicity control standards:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER) via Bonferroni Correction:}
    \begin{equation}
        p_{\mathrm{Bonf}} = \min(1.0, m \cdot p_{\mathrm{raw}})
    \end{equation}
    guaranteeing strict family-wise error rate control at level $\alpha = 0.05$.
    \item \textbf{False Discovery Rate (FDR) via Benjamini--Hochberg (BH):}
    \begin{equation}
        q_{\mathrm{FDR}} = \min_{k \ge i} \left(\frac{m}{k} p_{(k)}\right)
    \end{equation}
    controlling the expected proportion of false positive discoveries at level $\alpha = 0.05$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Mathematical Proofs and Algorithmic Specifications}
\label{sm:proof}
\label{sm:pseudocode}

\begin{theorem}[$\mathrm{E}(3)$ Equivariance of Q-PRISM Layer]
\label{thm:equivariance}
Let $\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x} \mathbf{Q}^T + \mathbf{g}$ denote an arbitrary continuous Euclidean transformation with rotation/reflection $\mathbf{Q} \in \mathrm{O}(3)$ and translation $\mathbf{g} \in \mathbb{R}^3$.\footnote{We adopt a row-vector convention throughout: coordinates are stored as $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 3}$ (row vectors), so the transformation is $\mathbf{x} \mathbf{Q}^T + \mathbf{g}$ rather than $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{g}$. This matches PyTorch's default batch-of-row-vectors layout.} Then for the Q-PRISM layer defined in main text Eqs.~(\ref{main:eq:coord_update}--\ref{main:eq:feat_update}):
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{h}_i^{l+1}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x}), \mathbf{h}) &= \mathbf{h}_i^{l+1}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{h}) \quad (\text{Invariance}) \\
    \mathbf{x}_i^{l+1}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x}), \mathbf{h}) &= \mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x}_i^{l+1}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{h})) \quad (\text{Equivariance})
\end{align}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Under transformation $\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{g}}$, the relative coordinate vector transforms as $\mathbf{r}_{ij}' = (\mathbf{x}_j\mathbf{Q}^T + \mathbf{g}) - (\mathbf{x}_i\mathbf{Q}^T + \mathbf{g}) = \mathbf{r}_{ij}\mathbf{Q}^T$, yielding invariant squared Euclidean distance:
\begin{equation}
    d_{ij}' = \|\mathbf{r}_{ij}'\|^2 = (\mathbf{r}_{ij}\mathbf{Q}^T)(\mathbf{r}_{ij}\mathbf{Q}^T)^T = \mathbf{r}_{ij}\mathbf{Q}^T\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{r}_{ij}^T = \|\mathbf{r}_{ij}\|^2 = d_{ij}
\end{equation}
by the orthogonality property $\mathbf{Q}^T\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{I}$.

The Gaussian RBF features $\boldsymbol{\phi}(d_{ij})$ are scalar functions of the invariant squared distance $d_{ij}$ and are thus invariant. The 3-body mean bond angles $\bar{\theta}_{ij}$ depend exclusively on inner products of unit relative vectors (main text Eq.~\ref{main:eq:angle}), which satisfy:
\begin{equation}
    (\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}\mathbf{Q}^T) \cdot (\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ik}\mathbf{Q}^T) = \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}\mathbf{Q}^T\mathbf{Q}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ik}^T = \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ik}
\end{equation}
by orthogonality of $\mathbf{Q}$. Because all inputs $\mathbf{u}_{ij}^{(s)} = [\mathbf{h}_i^p, \mathbf{h}_j^p, \boldsymbol{\phi}(d_{ij}), \bar{\theta}_{ij}]$ to the edge heads are strictly $\mathrm{O}(3)$-invariant, the parameterized quantum state $|\psi_{ij}^{(s)}\rangle$ and all resulting measurement observables ($\mathbf{m}_{ij}$, $\phi_x$) are invariant. The coordinate gating function $\phi_x(\mathbf{m}_{ij}) = \tanh(\mathbf{W}_g [\phi_x^{(1)}, \phi_x^{(2)}]^T)$ is a scalar function of invariant quantities and is thus invariant.

Substituting into the coordinate update equation:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{x}_i^{l+1}(\mathbf{x}', \mathbf{h}) &= \mathbf{x}_i' + \frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(i)|} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \mathbf{r}_{ij}' \cdot \phi_x(\mathbf{m}_{ij}) \nonumber \\
    &= (\mathbf{x}_i \mathbf{Q}^T + \mathbf{g}) + \left[\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(i)|} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \mathbf{r}_{ij} \phi_x(\mathbf{m}_{ij})\right] \mathbf{Q}^T \nonumber \\
    &= \left(\mathbf{x}_i + \frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(i)|} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \mathbf{r}_{ij} \phi_x(\mathbf{m}_{ij})\right) \mathbf{Q}^T + \mathbf{g} \nonumber \\
    &= \mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x}_i^{l+1}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{h}))
\end{align}
Node feature updates $\mathbf{h}_i^{l+1}$ depend solely on invariant messages $\mathbf{m}_{ij}$ and invariant node states $\mathbf{h}_i^l$, establishing exact feature invariance.\footnote{Our $\mathrm{E}(3)$ equivariance formulation treats all spatial vectors as polar vectors. For physical observables transforming as pseudovectors or axial tensors under improper rotations (e.g., magnetic dipole moments, angular momentum), the model would require parity-aware equivariant representations~\cite{batatiaMACEHigherOrder2022}. All QM9 molecular targets examined in this work are scalar invariants and are unaffected by this distinction.}
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Continuous Symmetry and Equivariance Stress Test.}
To empirically verify mathematical invariance and equivariance under physical coordinate transformations, we subjected Q-PRISM, classical EGNN, and a Non-Equivariant GNN control to continuous $360^\circ$ spatial rotations around arbitrary 3D axes and $1{,}000$ randomized $\mathrm{E}(3)$ transformations ($\mathbf{x} \mapsto \mathbf{x}\mathbf{Q}^T + \mathbf{g}$).

As demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:invariance}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Q-PRISM (Ours):} Coordinate equivariance error $= 2.38 \times 10^{-7}$, feature invariance error $= 2.38 \times 10^{-7}$, and global prediction invariance error mean $= 3.96 \times 10^{-8}$\,eV (maximum $5.66 \times 10^{-8}$\,eV across $1{,}000$ randomized $\mathrm{E}(3)$ transformations), confirming strict machine-precision symmetry preservation.
    \item \textbf{Classical EGNN:} Mean invariance error $= 6.50 \times 10^{-8}$\,eV (maximum $1.71 \times 10^{-7}$\,eV).
    \item \textbf{Non-Equivariant GNN:} Mean invariance error $= 2.92$\,eV (maximum $13.34$\,eV), exhibiting catastrophic symmetry failure.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Algorithmic Specifications.}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:prism} provides the complete algorithmic specification for a single forward pass of the PRISM layer. Algorithm~\ref{alg:qprism_edge} specifies the parameterized quantum edge operator of Q-PRISM that substitutes line~\ref{alg:prism_edge} in Algorithm~\ref{alg:prism}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{PRISM Forward Pass (Single Layer)}
\label{alg:prism}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Molecular graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{h})$
\REQUIRE Feature projection matrices $\mathbf{W}_p^{(s)} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times d_{\mathrm{node}}}$ per head $s \in \{1,2\}$
\REQUIRE SIREN parameters $\mathbf{W}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times d_{\mathrm{edge}}}$, $\mathbf{b}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$, $\mathbf{W}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{8 \times d_h}$ ($d_{\mathrm{edge}} = 13, d_h = 4$) with $\mathbf{W}_1 \sim \mathcal{U}(-1/d_{\mathrm{edge}}, 1/d_{\mathrm{edge}})$ per head $s \in \{1,2\}$
\REQUIRE Frequency scale $\omega_0 = 30.0$
\REQUIRE Coordinate gating vector $\mathbf{W}_g \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 2}$
\FOR{each edge $(i, j) \in \mathcal{E}$}
    \STATE $d_{ij} \leftarrow \|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|^2$ \COMMENT{Invariant squared distance}
    \STATE $\boldsymbol{\phi}(d_{ij}) \leftarrow [\exp(-\gamma_k(d_{ij} - \mu_k)^2)]_{k=1}^K$ \COMMENT{RBF encoding}
    \STATE $\bar{\theta}_{ij} \leftarrow \text{MeanAngle}(i, j, \mathcal{N}(i))$ \COMMENT{main text Eq.~\ref{main:eq:angle}}
    \FOR{each head $s \in \{1, 2\}$}
        \STATE $\mathbf{h}_i^{p} \leftarrow \mathbf{W}_p^{(s)} \mathbf{h}_i$ \COMMENT{Project node state to 2D}
        \STATE $\mathbf{u}_{ij}^{(s)} \leftarrow [\mathbf{h}_i^p, \mathbf{h}_j^p, \boldsymbol{\phi}(d_{ij}), \bar{\theta}_{ij}]$ \COMMENT{$\in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{edge}}},\; d_{\mathrm{edge}} = 13$}
        \STATE $\mathbf{o}_{ij}^{(s)} \leftarrow \tanh(\mathbf{W}_2 \sin(\omega_0(\mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{u}_{ij}^{(s)} + \mathbf{b}_1)))$ \label{alg:prism_edge} \COMMENT{SIREN}
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE $\mathbf{m}_{ij} \leftarrow [[\mathbf{o}_{ij}^{(1)}]_{1:3}, [\mathbf{o}_{ij}^{(1)}]_{5:8},\; [\mathbf{o}_{ij}^{(2)}]_{1:3}, [\mathbf{o}_{ij}^{(2)}]_{5:8}]$ \COMMENT{Message $\in \mathbb{R}^{14}$}
    \STATE $\phi_x \leftarrow \tanh(\mathbf{W}_g [[\mathbf{o}_{ij}^{(1)}]_4, [\mathbf{o}_{ij}^{(2)}]_4])$ \COMMENT{Coord.\ scalar update $\phi_x$}
\ENDFOR
\FOR{each node $i \in \mathcal{V}$}
    \STATE $\mathbf{x}_i \leftarrow \mathbf{x}_i + \frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(i)|}\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} (\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j) \cdot \phi_x$
    \STATE $\mathbf{h}_i \leftarrow \mathbf{h}_i + \text{MLP}([\mathbf{h}_i,\; \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \mathbf{m}_{ij}])$
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{h}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Q-PRISM Edge Operator (substitutes Algorithm~\ref{alg:prism} line~\ref{alg:prism_edge})}
\label{alg:qprism_edge}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Edge input $\mathbf{u}_{ij}^{(s)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{edge}}}$ ($d_{\mathrm{edge}} = 13$), PQC parameters $\boldsymbol{\Theta}$
\STATE $|\psi\rangle \leftarrow |0\rangle^{\otimes 4}$
\FOR{$l = 1$ to $L$}
    \STATE $|\psi\rangle \leftarrow U_{\mathrm{feat}}(\mathbf{h}_i^p, \mathbf{h}_j^p)\,|\psi\rangle$ \COMMENT{$R_Y$ node encoding}
    \STATE $|\psi\rangle \leftarrow U_{\mathrm{dist}}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\phi}(d_{ij}))\,|\psi\rangle$ \COMMENT{$R_Z$ distance encoding}
    \STATE $|\psi\rangle \leftarrow U_{\mathrm{ang}}(\bar{\theta}_{ij})\,|\psi\rangle$ \COMMENT{$R_Y$ angular encoding}
    \STATE $|\psi\rangle \leftarrow U_{\mathrm{rot}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_l)\,|\psi\rangle$ \COMMENT{$R_x R_y R_z$ variational rotations}
    \STATE $|\psi\rangle \leftarrow U_{\mathrm{ent}}\,|\psi\rangle$ \COMMENT{Ring CNOT entanglement}
\ENDFOR
\STATE $\mathbf{o}_{ij}^{(s)} \leftarrow [\langle Z_q \rangle,\; \langle Z_q Z_{q+1} \rangle]_{q=0}^3$ \COMMENT{8 expectation values}
\RETURN $\mathbf{o}_{ij}^{(s)} \in [-1, 1]^8$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Mechanistic Origin of Advantage: Fourier Expressivity and Spectral Resonance}
\label{sm:mechanism_details}
\label{sm:spectral}
\label{sm:omega}
\label{sec:omega_sensitivity}
\label{sm:dequant_details}

\paragraph{Empirical Dequantization Framework.}
In this work, the term ``dequantization'' is used in the empirical sense established by Schreiber et al.~\cite{Schreiber:2022roq} and Bowles et al.~\cite{bowles2024better}: identifying the mathematical functional mechanism responsible for empirical quantum model performance and constructing an iso-parameter classical architecture that natively spans that hypothesis class. This empirical functional dequantization is distinct from asymptotic complexity-theoretic dequantization algorithms~\cite{Tang:2018lmw} that establish polynomial-time classical matrix sampling for non-classical quantum algorithms.
While data re-uploading PQCs are theoretically known to compute truncated Fourier series~\cite{Schuld:2020enb, landman2023approximating}, our primary contribution is identifying that this periodic inductive bias directly addresses the representational bottleneck of molecular message passing, and demonstrating that this advantage can be fully captured classically via periodic edge activations.

\paragraph{Internal Dimensionality and Representation Capacity.}
Although the 4-qubit statevector evolves in a 16-dimensional complex Hilbert space ($\mathbb{C}^{16}$), local Pauli measurements collapse the quantum output to real expectation values $\langle Z_q \rangle, \langle Z_i Z_j \rangle \in [-1, 1]$. To evaluate whether internal dimensionality influences downstream capacity, we benchmark classical models across hidden dimensions $d_h \in \{4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128\}$ (SM~\ref{sm:param_scaling}, Table~\ref{tab:param_scaling}). Across all capacity regimes, PRISM maintains a consistent $32\text{--}43\%$ performance margin over the Classical Twin, demonstrating that the advantage stems from periodic functional expressivity rather than statevector dimensionality.

\paragraph{Physical Validity of Periodic Coordinate Updates.}
Periodic activations operate on projected geometric embeddings $\omega_0(\mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{u}_{ij} + \mathbf{b}_1)$ rather than unbounded raw spatial coordinates. An outer hyperbolic tangent gate $\tanh$ strictly bounds the coordinate displacement update to $[-1, 1]$ (main text Eq.~\ref{main:eq:coord_update}), preventing oscillatory instability during iterative message passing. For extreme non-equilibrium configurations (such as bond dissociation pathways), empirical validation against unconstrained baselines remains recommended.

\paragraph{Choice of Sinusoidal Activation Function.}
Sinusoidal activations were selected based on two architectural principles: (i) single-qubit rotation gates natively generate Fourier series spanned by sine and cosine functions~\cite{Schuld:2020enb}, aligning the classical hypothesis class directly with the PQC parameterization; and (ii) SIREN weight initialization~\cite{sitzmann2020siren} provides stable variance preservation across deep periodic layers. Alternative periodic bases, such as Gabor wavelets or Fourier features, represent natural extensions for comparative study.

\subsection{Architectural Component Ablation Study}

To isolate the contribution of each architectural component in Q-PRISM, we selectively disable individual mechanisms:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Gaussian RBF Distance Encoding:} Removing RBF ($K=8$) and reverting to raw scalar distance degrades multi-frequency Fourier representation capacity.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Head Processing:} Reducing from 2 heads to 1 head halves the quantum message dimensionality.
    \item \textbf{Entanglement Correlators:} Removing two-qubit $\langle Z_i Z_j \rangle$ correlators reduces the readout observables from 8 to 4 per head.
    \item \textbf{Variational Depth:} Sweeping PQC depth across $L \in \{1, 2, 4\}$.
\end{enumerate}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ablations_v2}, incorporating two-qubit entanglement correlators $\langle Z_i Z_j \rangle$ reduces test MAE from $0.818$\,eV to $0.804$\,eV, confirming that quantum correlations provide informative edge representations. In depth sweeps, $L = 2$ achieves optimal calibration ($0.804$\,eV) compared to $L = 1$ ($0.820$\,eV), justifying our default architectural configuration.

\subsection{Functional Response Analysis: Expressive Capacity vs.\ Periodic Expressivity}

To visualize the mechanistic origin of periodic advantage, we extract edge-level response functions $f(\theta)$ from trained models by sweeping the 3-body bond angle $\theta \in [0^\circ, 180^\circ]$ at fixed interatomic distance ($d = 2.25$~\AA$^2$) and fixed node embeddings (training median).

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/figure_pqc_vs_prism_angular_response.png}
    \caption{\textbf{Edge Functional Response vs.\ 3-Body Bond Angle} ($\theta \in [0^\circ, 180^\circ]$, fixed $d = 2.25$~\AA$^2$). Panels 1--2: Entangled and Separable PQCs output rich, multi-frequency trigonometric polynomials spanning $[-1, 1]$. Panel 3: Classical $\tanh$-MLP outputs remain flat; at this parameter budget ($d_h = 4$), the network suffers from expressive capacity starvation. Panel 4: PRISM (SIREN) fully restores large-amplitude, non-saturating sinusoidal oscillations at identical parameter parity, replicating the PQC function class classically.}
    \label{fig:angular_response}
\end{figure*}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:angular_response}, both quantum circuits (entangled and separable) produce smooth, large-amplitude sinusoidal oscillations across the angular domain. Under the Fourier series framework of Schuld et al.~\cite{Schuld:2020enb} and Landman et al.~\cite{landman2023approximating}, each single-qubit rotation $R_Y(\theta) = \exp(-i \theta \sigma_y / 2)$ natively constructs Fourier basis elements $\cos(\theta)$ and $\sin(\theta)$ that compose into rich trigonometric polynomials across observable outputs.

The classical $\tanh$-MLP, by contrast, produces a flat response over the same angular range. At this compact parameter budget ($d_h = 4$), the attenuated angular response of the Classical Twin is governed by expressive capacity constraints rather than overparameterized Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) spectral bias~\cite{rahaman2019spectral}: a monotonic $\tanh$ activation requires multiple linear combinations to approximate a single oscillatory period, whereas a sinusoidal activation directly computes harmonic frequencies with a single parameter. With only 4 hidden units processing 13 mixed inputs, the $\tanh$-MLP lacks sufficient representational capacity to resolve high-frequency angular orbital overlap. This angular insensitivity explains why standard monotonic classical baselines struggle on electronic orbital properties, which depend fundamentally on periodic angular overlap integrals.

When we examine PRISM (SIREN), the periodic sinusoidal activation $\sin(\omega_0 \mathbf{W} \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b})$ directly supplies the compact oscillatory basis that the $\tanh$-MLP lacks~\cite{sitzmann2020siren}. The classical SIREN head outputs the same large-amplitude, non-saturating oscillatory polynomials as the PQC, fully restoring angular sensitivity without requiring quantum circuits or statevector simulation.

This empirical correspondence reflects the practical implications of the trainability-simulability dilemma~\cite{Cerezo:2023nqf}: shallow parameterized circuits operating in trainable regimes reside within classically accessible hypothesis classes. PRISM provides an explicit classical architecture that operationalizes this hypothesis class within message passing. While these findings demonstrate empirical functional parity on molecular targets, formal complexity-theoretic classification of periodic graph hypothesis spaces remains an open question for theoretical study.

\paragraph{SIREN Initialization vs.\ Activation Dynamics.}
The performance contribution decomposes into activation expressivity and initialization dynamics: the $2 \times 2$ factorial ablation (main text Table~\ref{main:tab:init_ablation}) shows that sinusoidal activations contribute the primary error reduction ($-0.16\text{--}0.18$\,eV on Gap), with SIREN initialization providing a reinforcing interaction. In addition, the poor performance of Random Fourier Features (main text Table~\ref{main:tab:main_results}) demonstrates that learned, task-aligned harmonic frequencies are required rather than static periodic projections.

\section{Analytical Hardware Noise Models and NISQ Feasibility}
\label{sm:analytical_noise_scaling}

\paragraph{Hardware Characterization Scope \& Simulation Constraints.}
\textit{All device characterizations in this work are based on analytical and calibrated noise models evaluated via statevector simulation.}
Physical execution of the hybrid graph architecture on current quantum hardware faces three operational bottlenecks: (i) planar heavy-hex qubit topologies require SWAP routing for ring CNOT structures, expanding effective circuit depth by $1.5\text{--}2.0\times$; (ii) edge-level circuit evaluation requires evaluating $4{,}000\text{--}8{,}000$ quantum circuits per molecule per epoch, creating prohibitive execution latency on queued cloud QPUs; and (iii) multi-qubit fluxonium ring processors remain in experimental development. Consequently, calibrated analytical noise modeling provides the standard methodology for assessing algorithmic robustness to noise. The performance projections below integrate published device parameters from recent superconducting co-designs~\cite{Kline:2025ecg, ding2023fluxonium, Bao:2021use} to establish theoretical operational bounds under realistic decoherence and crosstalk.

\subsection{NISQ Hardware Noise and Zero-Noise Extrapolation (ZNE)}

\paragraph{Noise Model \& Pauli Twirling Justification.}
Noise simulations use per-gate depolarizing channels $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{dep}}(\rho) = (1-\lambda)\rho + \frac{\lambda}{3}(X\rho X + Y\rho Y + Z\rho Z)$ with single-qubit error rate $\lambda$ and two-qubit error rate $2\lambda$. This represents the Pauli-twirled noise regime~\cite{Kim:2023bwr}, wherein randomized compiling maps arbitrary coherent and device-level systematic errors into stochastic Pauli channels calibrated via randomized benchmarking~\cite{Berg:2022ugn, Giurgica-Tiron:2020rcf}. The simulated parameter sweep $\lambda \in [10^{-5}, 5 \times 10^{-2}]$ benchmarks architectural resilience across noise scales, spanning proposed ultra-low-noise superconducting systems such as the arm qubit ($\lambda \sim 10^{-5}$~\cite{Kline:2025ecg}) to contemporary transmon QPUs ($\lambda \sim 10^{-3}$~\cite{Kim:2023bwr}).

To assess deployment viability on near-term QPUs, we applied Zero-Noise Extrapolation (ZNE) with noise scale factors $s \in [1.0, 3.0]$ \cite{Temme:2016vkz, Giurgica-Tiron:2020rcf}.

As demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:zne}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item At standard gate error rates ($\lambda = 0.005$), Degree-2 Polynomial ZNE reduces expectation error from $0.0209$ to $0.0005$ ($97.7\%$ error reduction).
    \item At elevated noise ($\lambda = 0.010$), ZNE reduces error from $0.0387$ to $0.0031$ ($92.0\%$ error reduction).
    \item At severe $5\%$ noise ($\lambda = 0.050$), ZNE recovers approximately $32.5\%$ of expectation fidelity.
\end{itemize}
Fidelity recovery declines sharply at higher noise rates, indicating that practical NISQ deployment requires error mitigation beyond polynomial ZNE when $\lambda > 0.02$.

    \centering

    \caption{\textbf{NISQ Hardware Noise \& Zero-Noise Extrapolation (ZNE) Error Mitigation:} (a) Expectation value estimation error across depolarizing noise rates $\lambda \in [0.0, 0.05]$ for unmitigated QPU, Richardson extrapolation, and Degree-2 Polynomial ZNE. (b) Single-observable ZNE extrapolation trajectory ($s \to 0$) under $\lambda = 0.035$ depolarizing noise, recovering the ideal noiseless target.}

\paragraph{Intensive vs.\ Extensive Property Noise Scaling.}
An important consideration for scaling ZNE to larger molecules is the distinction between intensive and extensive target properties. Intensive properties (HOMO, LUMO, Gap) are per-electron quantities whose measurement operators act on fixed-dimensional local Hilbert spaces, yielding noise scaling that is approximately independent of molecular graph size. In contrast, extensive properties ($U_0$, $\alpha$) require summation over atom-centered contributions, where measurement noise accumulates as $\sigma_{\mathrm{ext}} \propto \sqrt{N_{\mathrm{atoms}}}$ under independent shot noise. For the small molecules in QM9 ($N_{\mathrm{atoms}} \le 29$), this distinction is modest, but for macromolecular systems the ZNE overhead for extensive targets would scale unfavorably unless combined with locality-aware observable decomposition strategies.

\paragraph{Device-Realistic Noise Validation.}
To validate the analytical depolarizing model against physical device architectures, we compared expectation degradation against a calibrated IBM Brisbane (Eagle r3, 127-qubit) noise model simulated via Qiskit Aer FakeBackend~\cite{Kim:2023bwr}. The Brisbane model incorporates empirical device metrics: qubit-specific $T_1$ ($158$--$394\,\mu$s), $T_2$ ($49$--$386\,\mu$s), per-gate error rates (SX: $\sim 2.9 \times 10^{-4}$; ECR: $\sim 1.1 \times 10^{-2}$), and readout assignment errors ($\sim 2.4\%$). Across $N = 50$ random circuit evaluations at $10^5$ shots, the calibrated Brisbane noise yields a mean expectation error $|\Delta\langle Z \rangle| = 0.0104$, compared to $0.0057$ for the uniform depolarizing model at $\lambda = 10^{-3}$ ($1.83\times$ offset). The strong Pearson correlation across per-circuit error distributions ($r = 0.87$, $p < 10^{-4}$) confirms that the depolarizing model faithfully preserves the relative structural error hierarchy of Pauli-twirled physical hardware.

\subsection{Theoretical Device Viability and Arm Qubit Crossing Analysis}

Dual-mode superconducting designs such as the arm qubit~\cite{Kline:2025ecg} change projected operating bounds for variational circuits. Published arm qubit parameters include two-qubit CZ infidelity of $8.6 \times 10^{-5}$ ($17$\,ns gate time), single-qubit gate infidelity below $10^{-5}$ ($5$\,ns), parasitic $ZZ$ crosstalk below $0.4$\,kHz, readout error $\sim 10^{-4}$, and data-mode coherence $T_1 \approx 300$--$420\,\mu$s ($T_2^E > 380\,\mu$s). These metrics represent tenfold to hundredfold improvements over contemporary transmon processors ($\lambda \sim 10^{-3}$, $ZZ \sim 10$--$100$\,kHz, readout error $\sim 10^{-2}$).

\paragraph{Circuit Execution Time Analysis.}
Our 4-qubit Q-PRISM PQC with $L = 2$ variational layers comprises $\sim 8$ single-qubit gates and $\sim 4$ CNOT (CZ-equivalent) gates per layer, yielding a total gate count of $\sim 24$ operations. On the arm qubit processor:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{\mathrm{arm}} \approx 16 \times 5\,\mathrm{ns} + 8 \times 17\,\mathrm{ns} = 216\,\mathrm{ns}
\end{equation}
compared to IBM transmon execution $\tau_{\mathrm{ibm}} \approx 1$--$2\,\mu$s. The circuit-to-coherence ratio becomes:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\tau_{\mathrm{arm}}}{T_2^E} = \frac{216\,\mathrm{ns}}{380\,\mu\mathrm{s}} \approx 5.7 \times 10^{-4}
\end{equation}
implying that Q-PRISM's PQC occupies $< 0.06\%$ of the coherence window, virtually eliminating decoherence-induced errors during circuit execution.

\paragraph{Arm Qubit Crossing-Point Analysis.}
Evaluating QM9 test edge representations across an extended noise sweep down to $\lambda = 10^{-5}$ reveals an operational threshold at $\lambda^* \approx 1.1 \times 10^{-4}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:arm_crossing}). Below $\lambda^*$, unmitigated expectation error falls below the high-precision observable threshold ($\epsilon < 10^{-3}$, corresponding to $> 99.9\%$ observable fidelity). Within the projected arm qubit regime ($\lambda \in [10^{-5}, 10^{-4}]$), hardware noise introduces negligible estimation bias relative to classical representation error, rendering Zero-Noise Extrapolation unnecessary. Conversely, in the transmon regime ($\lambda \sim 10^{-3}$), unmitigated error rises to $\sim 8.7 \times 10^{-3}$, requiring polynomial ZNE to restore sub-$10^{-3}$ precision. These projections assume isotropic depolarizing channels; physical device implementations will encounter additional coherent drift and spatial parameter inhomogeneity.

Only the unconstrained higher-capacity EGNN Standard ($3{,}856$ parameters) meaningfully models the Coulomb expansion ($0.6137 \times 10^{-2}$).

This experiment provides an informative negative control: Coulomb $N$-body mechanics involves extensive, non-periodic, unbounded trajectory drift governed by $1/r^2$ accelerations. Unlike localized quantum molecular systems where wavefunctions exhibit periodic angular symmetries and bounded radial transitions ($d \in [0.9, 2.5]$\,\AA), macroscopic classical mechanics lacks intrinsic periodic structure. Consequently, neither classical sinusoidal activations nor quantum PQC data re-uploading provide inductive advantage over unconstrained polynomial coordinates. However, Q-PRISM exhibits no divergent or oscillatory instability across 1{,}000 integration steps, confirming that the $\tanh$-gated coordinate update effectively bounds the periodic channel even outside its optimal physical domain (Figure~\ref{fig:nbody_traj}).

\paragraph{The Trainability-Simulability Dilemma.}
Table~\ref{tab:resources} summarizes the resource footprint and theoretical complexity across the baseline hierarchy.
Our empirical finding that PRISM (classical SIREN) matches and surpasses the 4-qubit PQC is grounded in recent quantum computational complexity theory.
Cerezo, Holmes et al.~\cite{Cerezo:2023nqf} proved that in variational quantum machine learning, the absence of barren plateaus is inextricably linked to classical simulability: any PQC that avoids barren plateaus via shallow circuit depth ($L \in \mathcal{O}(\log n)$) and local observables necessarily resides in a classically simulable hypothesis class.
In Q-PRISM, the edge PQC uses 4 qubits, $L = 2$ layers, and local single-qubit and nearest-neighbor correlator observables.
While this guarantees non-vanishing gradients and stable training, it simultaneously bounds the output to a low-dimensional truncated Fourier series~\cite{landman2023approximating, Schuld:2020enb}.
Conversely, attempting to escape classical simulability by scaling to deep, globally entangled circuits triggers exponential concentration of measure (Thanasilp et al.~\cite{Thanasilp:2022agd}), wherein quantum kernels concentrate to trivial constants and gradients vanish as $\mathcal{O}(2^{-n/2})$.
Thus, within the trainable regime for geometric graphs, quantum circuits provide no asymptotic expressivity separation over properly initialized classical periodic networks.

\paragraph{Macromolecular Scaling ($O(N)$).}
For molecules with $N > 50$ atoms, the fully-connected edge construction $O(N^2)$ becomes a bottleneck. Our implementation includes a $k$-nearest neighbor sparse graph builder that reduces edge complexity to $O(kN) = O(N)$ for constant $k$, enabling both Q-PRISM and PRISM to scale to macromolecular systems (proteins, polymers) without fundamentally altering the edge operator architecture.
For PRISM, each edge evaluation is a lightweight classical matrix multiplication and sine evaluation, executing in microseconds on modern GPUs without shot noise or quantum hardware latency.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Research Directions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Capacity scaling and foundation model regimes:} Our parameter scaling experiments (Section~\ref{sm:param_scaling}, Table~\ref{tab:param_scaling}) establish that the periodic advantage widens monotonically up to the standard medium-capacity regime of $100{,}000$ parameters (XXL, $100{,}127$ parameters, reaching a $51.3\%$ advantage on Gap over the Classical Twin). Nevertheless, evaluating periodic activations on ultra-large foundation models (e.g., MACE~\cite{batatiaMACEHigherOrder2022} at $\ge 1\mathrm{M}$ parameters) and large-scale catalytic datasets (e.g., OC20, ANI-1x) remains future work.
    \item \textbf{3-body angular pooling:} To maintain linear $O(E)$ complexity, our architecture averages angles over third atoms: $\bar{\theta}_{ij}$ (main text Eq.~\ref{main:eq:angle}). While computationally efficient, this averaging discards individual triplet geometry, unlike DimeNet++~\cite{klicperaFastUncertaintyAwareDirectional2020} or GemNet~\cite{klicperaGemNetUniversalDirectional2021}. Future work may explore per-triplet periodic projections.
    \item \textbf{Scalar-only geometric representation:} Both Q-PRISM and PRISM project 3D directional geometry into scalar invariants (interatomic distances $d_{ij}$ and averaged bond angles $\bar{\theta}_{ij}$), discarding per-edge directional vector information. As a consequence, the models cannot distinguish chiral enantiomers or capture higher-order multipolar interactions that require explicit vector or tensor equivariant channels~\cite{schuttEquivariantMessagePassing2021, batatiaMACEHigherOrder2022}. Extending PRISM to coupled scalar-vector message passing (analogous to PaiNN) or higher-order tensor equivariant architectures (analogous to MACE) is a natural future direction.
    \item \textbf{PRISM-PaiNN hybrid architecture:} The complementary performance profiles of PRISM (superior on HOMO, Dipole, Polarizability) and PaiNN (superior on LUMO, Gap) suggest a natural architectural synthesis: replacing SiLU activations in PaiNN's scalar interaction branch with SIREN periodic activations while preserving vector equivariant update channels. Since the periodic activation acts only on the invariant scalar pathway, this requires no modification to PaiNN's equivariance guarantees. A practical consideration is gradient stability: the Lipschitz constant of $\sin(\omega_0 x)$ scales as $\omega_0$, so the scalar-to-vector gating mechanism must accommodate amplified gradients. We leave experimental validation of this hybrid to future work.
    \item \textbf{Hardware execution and topology constraints:} All quantum circuits were simulated via PennyLane statevector backends; no physical 4-qubit fluxonium ring processor currently exists in commercial hardware. Scaling beyond 4 qubits on planar superconducting lattices (such as IBM's heavy-hex) also introduces SWAP routing overhead, increasing effective circuit depth by roughly $1.5$--$2.0\times$.
    \item \textbf{NISQ execution overhead and hybrid training:} Sequential QPU edge evaluations incur measurement shot noise and latency ($3$--$12$\,s per molecule). Near-term deployment could benefit from hybrid training: pre-training the equivariant backbone classically with PRISM, followed by quantum fine-tuning on targeted subsets to minimize QPU execution time.
    \item \textbf{Noiseless training assumption:} Hardware noise was characterized post-hoc via ZNE and analytical noise projections. Noise-aware training~\cite{cincio2021machine, Sharma:2020qjq} incorporating device-calibrated density matrices via PennyLane's \texttt{default.mixed} device represents a natural next step for experimental deployment.
    \item \textbf{Circuit depth and qubit register limits:} Our extended evaluations confirm that the classical dequantization result holds across deeper circuits up to $L = 16$ layers (Section~\ref{sm:depth_scaling}, Table~\ref{tab:depth_scaling}), where Q-PRISM asymptotically converges to exact parity with PRISM ($p = 0.969$), and across qubit registers up to $n_q = 8$ qubits (Section~\ref{sm:qubit_scaling}, Table~\ref{tab:qubit_scaling}), where PRISM maintains statistical superiority ($p < 0.025$) alongside a $540\times$ speedup. Whether circuits with non-local global Pauli observables or non-planar all-to-all entanglement topologies can break this parity remains an open theoretical question, though the trainability-simulability dilemma~\cite{Cerezo:2023nqf} suggests that any non-concentrating, trainable architecture remains within classically simulable complexity classes.
\end{enumerate}
